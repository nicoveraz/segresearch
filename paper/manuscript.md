## Abstract

Byte-level language models such as the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) group bytes into patches and run their large global model once per patch. BLT starts a patch where a small model's next-byte entropy is high. We show that under tight patch budgets this rule has a blind spot: positions whose *type* the preceding text fixes but whose *value* must be computed, such as the number after `= ` in a worked math solution. A nearly certain digit keeps the next byte's entropy near or below $\log_2 10 = 3.32$ bits, about BLT-1B's threshold for a 10% budget. In Meta's BLT-1B with patch starts on 10% of bytes, the entropy rule puts a patch start at 16% of the computed results in GSM8K solutions and gets 19.0% of them exactly right given the true preceding text; a boundary after each `=` at the same patch count gets 51.8%, and entropy combined with a label-free *boundary-dependence* signal gets 67.1% (default layout at 26% of bytes: 76.8%). The gap survives adapting BLT-1B to the budget with low-rank adapters (32.9% vs 72.7%, three runs per rule). In byte models trained from scratch at a 10% budget on a 1.9 GB math corpus, it is not detectable at 1M parameters, appears on final answers at 12M and on both targets at 50M, as training data grow too: at 50M boundary dependence gets 35.9% of computed results against 13.9% for entropy and 78.9% of final answers against 59.0% (3 seeds each), although final answers mostly copy the solution's last number and entropy starts a patch at every one. In BLT-1B the blind spot narrows at larger budgets (entropy covers about 90% of the values right after `= ` at 20% of bytes) and in the same solutions rewritten as prose, where a patch start at each value still pays. Boundary dependence, the change in the model's own loss when a patch start is removed or added, measured per two-byte context on text in the same format, finds these positions without labels.

## 1. Introduction

Byte-level models avoid a fixed tokenizer by working on raw bytes, and recover efficiency by grouping bytes into variable-length patches [@pagnoni2025blt; @slagle2024spacebyte; @hwang2025hnet]. In BLT, a small byte-level language model estimates the entropy of each next byte; a patch starts where that entropy crosses a threshold, and the large global transformer runs once per patch. The intuition is that compute should go where prediction is hard.

Entropy measures uncertainty about the next byte. What a patch start actually provides is different: a fresh global state for the bytes that follow. These coincide in prose, where a surprising byte often begins a new word. They come apart at a computed result. After `16 - 3 - 4 = `, the next byte is almost certainly a digit, so its entropy stays near or below $\log_2 10 = 3.32$ bits however unsure the model is about which digit (BLT-1B's patcher is unsure there, at a median of 2.8–3.0 bits). At a 10% budget BLT-1B's entropy threshold sits about that high, so it spends patches on hard-to-predict prose and skips most of these positions (§5).

We make four contributions:

1. **A blind spot in a trained 1B model.** At a 10% patch budget, BLT-1B's own entropy rule skips most computed results in GSM8K solutions, and about half at 15%; forcing a boundary after each `=` at the same patch count raises their exact match, given the true preceding text, by 19–33 points (§4). The cue before a result caps its entropy near BLT-1B's threshold at a 10% budget; larger budgets and prose without `=` narrow the blind spot (§5).
2. **The gap survives training and adaptation.** BLT-style models trained from scratch at 10–20% budgets show the same ordering across 3 seeds. Trained at 1M, 12M and 50M parameters on a 1.9 GB math corpus, with training data growing alongside, the label-free trigger's advantage over entropy is not detectable at 1M and appears at 12M (+10 points on final answers) and 50M (+20 on final answers, which mostly copy the solution's last number, and +22 on computed results; §6). BLT-1B adapted to a 10% budget with low-rank adapters keeps a 40-point gap (§8).
3. **A label-free trigger.** *Boundary dependence*, the change in the model's own loss when a patch start is removed or added, averaged per preceding two-byte context, finds computed results without labels or hand-written rules, given text in the same format to fit it; it learns the contexts that precede values (§7).
4. **Scope.** The effect needs a computed value and a model able to compute it; it is largest after a cue that fixes the value's type and, in BLT-1B, at tight budgets, while in our small models it persists at 15–25% (§5, §6). Copied names and values and logic lookups gain 1–3 points from a patch start at 10%, and values the model cannot compute show no detectable gain (−0.3 points, −1.1 to +0.5; §9). Scratchpad Patching's entropy trigger shows no detectable difference from random scratchpads on final answers (−0.3 points, −2.2 to +1.6; §10).

## 2. Background and related work

**Patching rules.** BLT [@pagnoni2025blt] describes two entropy rules: a global threshold, $H(x_t) > \theta_g$, and an approximate monotonic rule that starts a patch where entropy rises, $H(x_t) - H(x_{t-1}) > \theta_r$. Its final version compares both at 8B scale and defaults to the global threshold when not specified, as do the released BLT-1B checkpoint and the official code (`monotonicity: false`, threshold 1.335); we call that rule *entropy* and the monotonic rule *jump*. BLT also notes that an entropy model with a small receptive field can be stored as a lookup table, and its Figure 3 illustrates patching with a small entropy model that sees two bytes; our dependence rule (§7) is such a table with a different target. Entropy spikes as boundaries go back to dynamic pooling [@nawrot2023dynamic]. SpaceByte [@slagle2024spacebyte] starts patches at word boundaries; H-Net [@hwang2025hnet], AU-Net [@videau2025aunet], MrT5 [@kallini2024mrt5] and ByteSpan [@goriely2025bytespan] learn or derive boundaries in other ways. AU-Net reports GSM8K accuracy as a benchmark, rising with the number of hierarchy stages; none of these papers examines where boundaries fall on computed outputs or measures answer accuracy as a function of where boundaries are placed.

**Compute between patches.** Scratchpad Patching [@zheng2026scratchpad] decouples compute from patch size by inserting transient global steps ("scratchpads") inside patches, triggered by an absolute next-byte entropy threshold, and names the stale-context problem *patch lag*. Its trigger ablation compares strategies on validation bits per byte (code, natural-language and math splits) and finds the entropy trigger best; its downstream evaluation (code pass@1, multiple-choice understanding) includes no math answer accuracy. Fast BLT [@kallini2026fastblt] speeds up decoding without changing where boundaries go. EntropyMoE [@liu2026entropymoe] uses patch entropy to route each patch of a BLT-style model to feed-forward experts; it is evaluated on bits per byte and downstream accuracy, not on math.

**Tokenization and arithmetic.** For static tokenizers, how numbers are segmented affects arithmetic [@singh2024tokenization; @meister2026tokeval]. Our setting differs: boundaries are decided online by a model, and the question is which positions receive a fresh global step.

**Selection signals.** Rho-1 [@lin2024rho1] selects training tokens by excess loss against a reference model; we test a patch rule built on the same signal as a baseline (§12). Mixture-of-Depths [@raposo2024mod] routes compute per token by a learned router.

## 3. Setup

**BLT-1B.** We use the released BLT-1B through its Hugging Face conversion (`itazap/blt-1b-hf`), which accepts explicit patch lengths, so layouts can be changed at inference without retraining. The conversion drops BLT's 512-byte sliding window in the entropy patcher and in the local encoder and decoder (transformers issue 49185; it and the fix proposed in pull request 49188 were still open on 1 November 2026). We restore the window with that fix (applied to transformers 5.18; the patch is in our repository) and use the corrected model throughout, except where §13 reports the released conversion. Positions before byte 512 are unchanged by the fix, since the window covers every earlier byte; past it the released conversion over-segments, and 121 of our 294 test problems are longer than 512 bytes. With the window, the default layout starts a patch on 25.5% of bytes on GSM8K, against 28.4% without it. We build tight-budget layouts at R = 10% and 15% of bytes. Thresholds are fitted once on the first 300 GSM8K training problems (297 fit the context) so that the mean patch rate there is R, then applied position by position at test time; whether a byte starts a patch never depends on later bytes. (Choosing the top R% within each test problem instead gives the same conclusions; §13.)

**Layouts.** *entropy*: BLT-1B's next-byte entropy. *results* (hand-written): a boundary right after every `= `, the rest filled by entropy. *dep*: boundary dependence (§7). *entdep*: the sum of standardized entropy and dependence.

**Evaluation.** 294 GSM8K test problems (question, worked solution with calculator annotations removed, and "The final answer is N"), containing 796 in-line computed results: the number right after `= `, with its `$` if any (181 are written `= $N`). A target counts as exactly right when every byte is the model's top prediction given the true preceding text (teacher forcing), and as covered when a patch starts at its first byte. We report final answers as well. In GSM8K the final answer repeats the solution's last number in 94.6% of the 1,319 test problems (95.6% of our 294; thousands separators ignored), and that number ends a median of 8 bytes before the final-answer line, so under teacher forcing a final answer is mostly a copy. We test paired differences with exact McNemar tests and 95% intervals that resample whole problems.

**Small models trained from scratch.** A BLT-style model in MLX: a local encoder (byte embedding plus in-patch offset embedding), cross-attention pooling with the patch mean as query, a global transformer (D = 128, 4 layers unless noted; 1.1M parameters in all, 0.8M of them global), and a local decoder layer that attends to the previous 32 bytes across patch boundaries. Patch starts come from a fixed rule applied to the bytes and to a small entropy model (one layer, 32 dimensions) trained on the same corpus. Training: GSM8K and MATH training solutions [@cobbe2021gsm8k; @hendrycks2021math] (9.7 MB, GSM8K's calculator annotations kept), 32,000 steps of 32 windows of 128 bytes, AdamW with warmup and cosine decay. Evaluation: 660 GSM8K and 700 MATH test problems held out from training, with greedy exact match given the true prefix on 1,000 computed results (the number after `=` inside an annotation such as `<<16-3-4=9>>9`, copied after `>>`), 660 final answers (after `#### `) and 700 MATH `\boxed{}` answers; patch boundaries during decoding are decided online by the same rule. Three seeds per configuration; rules are compared by the difference of seed means with a Welch 95% interval over seeds (degrees of freedom rounded down, which widens it).

## 4. The blind spot in BLT-1B

At BLT-1B's default budget its patcher covers computed results well (79% of them start a patch), and removing the patch start at the final answer costs 19 points of exact match (97.0% $\rightarrow$ 78.3%), confirming that the boundary matters to BLT-1B, whose own layout starts a patch there. (That test scores the final answer as the solution last wrote it, with any `$` and thousands separators, on 300 problems; Table 1 scores the plain number after "The final answer is" on the 294 problems that fit the context, which gives 73.5% for the default layout.) Under a tight budget, entropy drops computed results faster than other positions: at 15% it covers 48% of them, at 10% only 16%.

## 5. What the blind spot depends on

Besides Table 1's 294 problems, this section uses 915 further GSM8K test problems (those among problems 300–1,318 that fit the context and had a valid prose rewrite, made for a measurement not reported here; 2,448 computed values) and a prose rewrite of 221 of the first 300 (711 values). On both, thresholds are fitted on the same 1,382 GSM8K training problems in the writing tested, and a value counts as covered when a patch starts at its first digit, after any `$`. (Table 1 counts the `$`: there the default layout starts a patch at only 20% of the 181 results written `= $N`, yet gets 71% of them right, against 78% of the others.)

**The cue caps entropy.** If a digit is certain to come next, the next byte's entropy cannot exceed $\log_2 10 = 3.32$ bits, however unsure the model is about which digit. BLT-1B's threshold for a 10% budget lies at about this level (3.36 bits), so after a cue that makes a digit nearly certain, entropy rarely starts a patch. In Table 1's results, entropy at 10% starts a patch at none of the 181 written `= $N` and at 21% of the 615 written `= N`. On the 915 problems the patcher gives a digit a median probability of 0.996 at the first digit after `= $` (median entropy 2.76 bits), where entropy at 10% starts no patch; after `= ` the median is 0.966 (3.00 bits) and entropy covers 18.9% of the values. Of all values it misses at 10%, 90.1% (88.4 to 91.8) have a digit probability of at least 0.9. Writing `is ` instead of `= ` before the 1,862 values written `= N` lowers that median from 0.966 to 0.736 and raises entropy's coverage of them from 18.9% to 65.7% at the same budget (+46.9 points, +44.5 to +49.3). We found the split by cue after the fact, in the prose experiment below, and measured these problems afterwards to test it.

**Budget.** In BLT-1B, as the budget grows the threshold falls below the ceiling and the blind spot closes: on the 915 problems entropy starts a patch at 14% of computed values at 10% of bytes, 56% at 15%, 89% at 20% and 97% at 25% (thresholds 3.36, 2.86, 2.38 and 1.90 bits), and after `= $` at 0%, 33%, 85% and 97%. It starts one at 94% of final answers already at 10%. BLT's own scaling experiments use average patches of 6 and 8 bytes [@pagnoni2025blt], about 17% and 12.5% of bytes; at 12.5% entropy covers 28% of computed values. In our models trained from scratch, whose entropy model has one layer of 32 dimensions, the gap persists at 15–25% budgets (§6).

**Format.** We also had an LLM rewrite the solutions as prose without `=` or arithmetic symbols between numbers, keeping every computed value in order with the same digits (checked automatically). At 10%, entropy starts a patch at 57.2% of the values in prose against 13.6% in the original (+43.6 points, +38.8 to +48.2). Adding a start at every value to entropy's layout, slightly beyond the budget, raises exact match by 37.0 points in the original (+33.1 to +41.1) but only by 12.9 in prose (+10.3 to +15.7): the blind spot narrows but does not disappear. Most of those 12.9 points come from the 163 values after `$`, a type-fixing cue the rewrite kept: entropy starts a patch at none of them (nor at any of the original's 167), and a start adds 36 points there, against 6 after a plain space, where entropy already covers 74%. A start at every value still pays in prose: against the same layout without those starts it adds 28.4 points (+24.7 to +32.2; 44.3 in the original). Values are harder in prose (default layout 56.8% against 77.9%; BLT-1B may have seen the original solutions in training), and prose has 1.43 times as many bytes per value, so the same budget gives it more patch starts per value; the `is ` swap above, which changes only the cue, is the cleaner test.

## 7. A label-free trigger: boundary dependence

The hand-written rule uses domain knowledge (`=`). We look for a signal that finds the same positions from the model alone.

**Definition.** For BLT-1B: on 400 GSM8K training problems, compute the per-byte loss under the default layout and under a 10% entropy layout. For each position t, take the mean rise in loss over bytes t…t+3, and average it per preceding two-byte context $(b_{t-2}, b_{t-1})$. At inference the score is a causal table lookup. The top contexts are arithmetic (`=$`, `0*`, `5=`, `3=`); after `= ` the mean rise is 0.88 nats against 0.13 on average.

For the small trained models the same recipe fails: a reference model trained with entropy patches rarely had a boundary at a result, so it never learned to use one, and removing one costs little. Training the reference model with random patch starts (25%) and measuring the drop in loss from *adding* one patch start at t to a random 10% layout fixes this; the top contexts then include `<<`, `=6`, `x=` and `>>`.

**Results.** On BLT-1B (Table 1), entropy plus dependence beats entropy on computed results by +29.5 points at 15% (+25.6 to +33.4) and +48.1 at 10%, and beats even the hand-written rule by +10.4 (+7.7 to +13.2): the table also places boundaries at operands and operators, not only after `=`. On final answers it beats entropy by +11.6 points at 10% (+6.5 to +16.7; $p = 3 \times 10^{-5}$) but not at 15% (+2.4, −1.0 to +5.8), and it is tied with the hand-written rule (+2.4, −2.4 to +6.8 at 10%). Entropy starts a patch at nearly every final answer at both budgets (93.5% and 98.3%); what differs is how many computed results it covers (16% and 48%). Dependence alone fails on final answers on BLT-1B (40.8% and 4.8%), because "The final answer is " is not an arithmetic context; in the trained models dependence alone scores higher on every seed (Table 2; entropy plus dependence: 9.7% / 22.5%, 2 seeds), though with two seeds the intervals include 0 (+7.4 points, −7.4 to +22.1; +44.9, −74.6 to +164.4).

To test whether BLT-1B can learn its way around the blind spot, we train low-rank adapters (rank 16, 16.9M parameters, on the global transformer and the decoder's cross-attention) for 1,500 steps on GSM8K training solutions under each 10% layout, three runs per rule. Each model is tested on the same 796 results under its own training layout.

## 9. Which positions are affected

If the blind spot is about computed values, copies and lookups should gain little from a patch start. We repeat the BLT-1B test on four other target types at 10% (train-fitted thresholds).

**Table 5.** BLT-1B at 10%, exact match. *Forced*: a patch start at every target, the rest by entropy.

{{table:table4_scope}}

These copies and lookups gain 1–3 points from a patch start (code: +1.4, $p = 4 \times 10^{-5}$), against 32.8 for computed results. This does not hold for copying in general: BLT-1B's final answers, mostly copies of the solution's last number, need a start at the answer (§4). Where BLT-1B cannot compute the value (program-trace arithmetic, 20.5% even with default patches) or answer at all (logic Yes/No answers at chance under every layout), placement has nothing to act on. Small models trained on the logic and trace tasks give the same picture: logic is not learned (answers at chance), and on traces, where each value's operands sit inside the local decoder's 32-byte window, entropy skips most values (83% at 10%), yet the hand-written rule is tied with it (21.8% vs 20.6%, 2 seeds; +1.2 points, 95% interval over seeds −23.1 to +25.5). With the window cut to 8 bytes the hand-written rule is 4.1 points higher (24.2% vs 20.1%, 3 seeds), with no detectable difference (−8.4 to +16.5). Our reading is that a start pays when a model able to compute the value computes it, possibly only when the operands lie beyond the local model's reach (the window test is inconclusive); type and task are also confounded in Table 5.

## 11. Compute

Fewer patches save global compute, but local layers run on every byte. For BLT-1B, counting matrix-multiply FLOPs, the local layers cost 299 MFLOPs per byte, the global model 2,653 MFLOPs per patch, and the entropy model 199 MFLOPs per byte. A 10% budget then costs 65% of the default's forward compute and 15% costs 76%. A dependence-table rule is a lookup and needs no entropy model: 48% at 10% and 59% at 15% (an entropy model stored as a lookup table, as BLT suggests, would also avoid that cost). On computed results, dependence alone at 15% is tied with the default layout (77.3% against 76.8%; +0.5 points, −1.7 to +2.7) at about 60% of its compute (Figure 3), but it loses 32.7 points on final answers (−38.1 to −27.2; §7). In our small models the local layers dominate, so a 10% budget saves only 24% of compute (Figure 4).

## 12. Negative results

**Spending patches on hard or learnable bytes does not help.** A Rho-1-style rule (patches where a small model's loss exceeds a reference model's) and simplified learned chunking lost to hand-placed boundaries in every setting tried.

**Finer arithmetic boundaries do not help.** Per-digit patches after `=` and operand-aligned patches looked promising at two seeds (on computed results and final answers); at five seeds neither differs detectably from a matched random control (+0.7 points, −1.2 to +2.6; +1.9, −2.9 to +6.7).

**A learned patcher does not beat the table.** A small network that predicts the dependence gain from the last 8 bytes, trained on math and on logic/trace data together, has held-out correlations close to each domain's own table (math 0.140 against 0.140; logic and traces 0.147 against 0.158) and covers 83% of computed-result starts (table: 37%), but trained into a model at 10% it gets 14.3% / 57.4% against the table's 17.1% / 67.4% (−2.7 points, −15.9 to +10.4; −10.0, −18.2 to −1.8).

**Token models are partly protected.** With a Qwen2.5-0.5B draft and a 1.5B target on generated program traces, the draft agrees with the target on only 53% of computed-value tokens (86% on text), so speculative decoding loses speed there; but the draft is rarely confident on them (6.5%), so a confidence-based early exit would let few errors through. On GSM8K, computed results are the easiest tokens for these models; we did not test why (overlap with their training data is one possibility). In token models a computed value's uncertainty shows in the model's own confidence; in byte patching, the decision to start a patch before a result is made from bytes whose type is predictable.

**The original synthetic result was mostly architectural.** In an earlier synthetic benchmark with sum pooling and a patch-local decoder, splitting a computation across patches cost up to a full bit; with cross-attention pooling and a decoder that sees across patch boundaries, most of that penalty disappeared. All results above use the cross-attention model.

## 13. Limitations

**Scale.** Models trained from scratch reach 53.7M parameters, on 1.5 billion bytes (the models of Table 2 have 1.1M; D = 64 is 0.2M). Parameters, training bytes and passes over the GSM8K and MATH solutions (about 1.4 at 1.1M, about 15 at 53.7M) grow together, three sizes on one corpus show a trend, not a law, and at 53.7M the gap on computed results is smaller with gradient clipping, and its interval then includes 0 (+14.3, −5.7 to +34.4, against +22.0, +13.6 to +30.4 without). The models are small in every part: a 128-byte context, one local decoder layer over 32 bytes and a one-layer, 32-dimensional entropy model. BLT-1B results come from inference-time layout changes and low-rank adaptation, not from full training at a tight budget. At 50M the hand-written rule has two seeds and the jump and double-budget results one each.

**Teacher forcing.** All accuracies score each target given the true preceding text, so a final answer is mostly a copy of the solution's last number (§3). End to end, BLT-1B cannot solve GSM8K by itself: even after adaptation it gets about 2% of final answers under every layout (measured with the released conversion), so the gaps above could not be measured end to end. The models trained from scratch also solve essentially no GSM8K problems end to end at any size (at most 1 of 100 for any run).

**Domain.** The effect is established for arithmetic in worked math solutions written with cues that fix the next byte's type (`= `, `$`), in BLT-1B at budgets of 10–15% of bytes (§5) and in models trained from scratch at 10–25% (§6); the models trained from scratch saw only GSM8K's annotated format. Code shows a small, significant effect; logic and program traces were inconclusive: the models lacked the skill, or the local model may already have seen the operands (§9).

**Label-free trigger.** The dependence table is fitted on the domain it is tested on and does not transfer across formats (the GSM8K table covers 0% of program-trace values; a table refitted on the scaling corpus covers no computed-result start, §6). It learns the contexts that precede values, so it inherits their format. On BLT-1B, the best variant (entropy plus dependence) differs from the one that scores higher on every seed in trained models (dependence alone; §7), which does not reach the hand-written rule. A follow-up [@verazuniga2026patch] used another two-byte table, fitted without labels to predict repeats of recent text, which on GSM8K works as a learned detector of the cue before values; its version 2 corrects the main claims of its first version.

**Checkpoint conversion.** The public BLT-1B conversion lacks the 512-byte sliding window; our BLT-1B results use it with the window restored by the fix proposed upstream (§3). With the released conversion the conclusions are the same, with different magnitudes (results − entropy at 15% and 10%: +25.0 and +26.6 points).

**Thresholds.** Our original BLT-1B layouts chose the top R% of positions within each test problem, so a position's selection could depend on later bytes. Train-fitted, position-by-position thresholds reproduce every conclusion, with similar or larger gaps (released conversion, results − entropy: +25.0 against +21.5 points at 15%, +26.6 against +27.1 at 10%).

## 14. Conclusion

Entropy tells a byte-level model where the next byte is surprising, not where its decoder needs the global model. The two diverge at computed outputs whose type the preceding text fixes: once only a digit can follow, the next byte's entropy stays near or below $\log_2 10$ bits, about BLT-1B's threshold at a 10% budget. Under tight budgets, entropy-triggered patching skips these positions and teacher-forced accuracy on them collapses: in a trained 1B model, after adaptation, and in models trained at the budget (1.1M parameters on the solutions alone, 53.7M on the larger corpus). In BLT-1B, larger budgets and cues that do not fix the type narrow the blind spot. Boundary dependence, a two-byte table fitted without labels on text in the same format, finds many of these positions by learning the contexts that precede them. Where such cues precede values and budgets are tight, patch starts should follow what the decoder needs from the global model rather than how surprising the next byte is.

## Appendix A. Experimental standards

The project ran as a partly autonomous research loop, and several early conclusions were wrong. The standards in `program.md` exist because breaking each produced a wrong result: a word-start rule that read the byte it was predicting (caught by an automatic causality test, `test_causal.py`); effects at two seeds that were not detectable at five (§12); a synthetic generator that placed dependencies outside the model's context; an effect that was mostly architectural; undertrained models hiding the effect; and a rule that exploited the generator's fixed number of variable names. Trained-model comparisons use three or more seeds except where a table or the text says otherwise: in Table 3, 1.1M dependence and 53.7M hand-written have two seeds, and jump and entropy at 20% one; in the text, entropy plus dependence (§7) and the window-32 trace models (§9) have two. BLT-1B comparisons use paired tests, and every rule is checked for causality.
